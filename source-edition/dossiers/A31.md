## A31-S04: Research correspondence

[████]

The operator’s questions, excerpted from the supplied correspondence:

In three sentences for an international reader, what do you believe Team Mirai is, and what is it not?

Many digital parties say they are restoring a broken representation link or feedback loop. What, in your view, is the specific thing Japanese party politics has lost that your group is trying to restore, and what mechanism is the most promising for that end?

What changed from “Team Anno (2024 Tokyo)” to “Team Mirai (party)”. What was the decisive political lesson that made a party necessary rather than only a project or a tool ecosystem?

How do you draw the boundary between Team Mirai and Digital Democracy 2030: what is shared, what is separated on purpose, and why does neutrality matter?

Which internal workflows are “digital-native” (membership, fundraising, candidate selection, policy drafting, consultation, communications), and which remain conventional?

Where does AI sit in the loop in daily practice, where is AI not used by principle? What is your practical definition of “human in the loop”?

Team Mirai is often described with terms like e-democracy, plurality, technocracy: which terms do you embrace, which do you resist, and what Japanese political problem do they name?

New parties often face a “hyper-leadership” risk where the leader becomes the party brand. How do you think about this in relation to Takahiro Anno’s role?

European readers will ask whether Mirai resembles political innovations of platform parties from our last 10-15 years like The Pirate Parties, Podemos, Syriza, or Movimento 5 Stelle. Does that comparison help (try asking AI about them, if you’re not familiar), and if not, what is a better comparison from your context?

What does “success” look like for you until 2030, and what should observers watch to evaluate it?
