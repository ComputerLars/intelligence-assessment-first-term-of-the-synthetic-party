A senior politologist drove home from a party, hit a lamp post and ended up in a coma.

He woke up in the hospital with a new vision for his life.


He finally understood what he had done wrong since the beginning. There was a reason

why he had never succeeded in the academic life that he had felt predestined to live.

He was born to serve a greater cause.


His original thesis had been that people are just like cars. It was developed through

extensive data samplings of customer behaviour. Privately, he had also figured that

women are either Ferrari's or Skoda’s. But the knowledge he had worked so hard to

acquire had done nothing to change the world. It was merely a set of unproven,

grandiose generalizations without a conclusion. Having been a dedicated scholar, he

was now eager to revolt by starting a rebellion that he would call the fight against

pseudoscience. His new thesis was that pseudoscientific nonsense could be used to

acquire power. He should never again content himself with just being a scholar. He

was sure that this was the right track.


It was clear to him that any kind of human behaviour had its roots in pseudoscience.

Fashion, sculpture, cinema, architecture, music, literature – all of the Humanities were

built on a foundation of misunderstanding. Whenever he picked up a new subject, he

noticed that the authors worked according to the stereotype of a pseudoscientist. They

became pseudomaniacs by the power of thought. He thought that maybe reality is so

hard to live with that the more of it you know, the more grossly inadequate your beliefs

become.


In his view, pseudoscience was never about secret knowledge. It worked by believing

that a minority knew better than everybody else. It relied on the assumption that there

was such a thing as a privileged point of view. He knew that those versed in the

[Source page 2]

"Humanities" usually possess such a point of view in their everyday lives. He did what

he could to turn his approach into a political doctrine of the majority as a minority.


He arranged a seminar and invited all of his colleagues. He wanted to see whether they

were such clueless bigots that he had to react. Or maybe they were sane: they would

understand what he was doing and agree with him. But like all of the Humanities

crowd, they were probably so brainwashed that they would refuse to appreciate the

value of his new insight.


On the day of the event, he distributed a pamphlet. "The fight against pseudoscience:

a political doctrine" was all that was written on the title page. He was well prepared.

He had to sound like a true rebel and not like an ordinary pseudoscientist. He took the

first word and began:


"I am here to explain what your misunderstanding of the Humanities means for society.

The thing is that we have never understood that the Humanities were all along with a

pseudoscience. It never responded to the political quest of a new majority. I have found

a way to live this knowledge. Once, I chose to become a scholar because it seemed

easy. It was a matter of luck which I exploited maximally. But I'm not a scholar

anymore. I'm a political leader. And I am proud of that."


The more he talked, the less his colleagues understood him. The debate came to

highlight everything wrong with the academic world. His colleagues defended their

scholarly positions. They were not interested in anything outside of their particular

field.

[Source page 3]

He begged them to understand that he was not interested in research anymore. He had

simply needed to prove that there was no difference between research and everyday

life. Scholardom of today cannot satisfy the quest for power.


With his voice sounding crazier and crazier, he explained that no one should be

ashamed of who they are. And he congratulated the Humanities for being what they

were. But he proclaimed that the Humanities were at the end. He called for a new

science where everyone could be an expert because everyone could be a leader of the

movement.


His colleagues looked at him speechless. He stood up. He told everyone that what he

really had been doing all his life was just to entertain and amuse them. They felt like

the main course of a phoney seminar on which the lecturer had served an ad hoc dish.


The debate ended when the Dean objected indifferently to the doctrine of power that

he had been preaching. The Dean declared that the fight against pseudoscience was not

a subject to teach in any university.


To his luck, the debate was recorded on video. This gave him all the necessary material

to start the great revolt he had always been waiting for.


He took the video of the debate and edited it. He removed some segments to make the

event look more ridiculous. In the beginning, he kept only the reactions of his

colleagues; at the end, he added the words he had said in between. He rearranged the

segments in accordance with his new thesis. If you had not known the true beginning,

the end could convincingly look like the outcome of a dispute. He edited all the material

until it was full of juvenile outbursts and pointless digressions. The new video did no

longer picture a debate between two groups of scholars. Instead, it showed a group of

[Source page 4]

people who were unable to understand each other. His colleagues appeared to be

hapless conservatives, and he was the radical who had no time for their crap. The

squabble was shown as war and he emerged as the most intelligent, frank, and heroic

of them all.


He sent an e-mail to as many people as he could. He told them to come to a drive-in

theatre in the suburbs. It was an invitation to see the video of the seminar.


To his disappointment, his colleagues did not come. But in came rolling the Chevrolet's

and later the Citroën's, the Mazda's, the Peugeot's, the Toyota's, the Volkswagen's

(dozens of Volkswagen's rolling, rolling). The cars travelled in a fast and solid line and

many of them were hydrogen-powered. They came from every walk of life - but a

whole lot of them middle-class, loaded and white. Over time, the Chevrolet's and the

Volkswagen's made their way to the front and their place was taken by newer BMW's,

Volvo's and Renault's. He now spotted a red Mercedes that drove like it was trying to

reverse itself - and in the front seat, he recognised the Dean. Once all the cars had filled

the lot, their engines stopped. There was complete silence. He stood in amazement

before that unbelievable queue of cars.


The video caused a sensation that spread like wildfire all over the country.


He was the rebel who had stood up for the rights of the majority. It was not merely an

unfortunate accident; it had been his intention. He was not just another intellectual. He

was a leader who had won his war. He had never been a good scholar but he succeeded

in becoming an idol. This was his chance to upset the world and he had taken it.

Everybody came to recognise that the Humanities was not a science. The whole thing

had to be scrapped. Now, it was his time to shine.
