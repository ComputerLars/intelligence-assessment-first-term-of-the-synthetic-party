David McClelland, the great Harvard psychologist and expert on motivation, says there are
three requirements for change.

In order to change:

1.  A person has to know how to change

       If a person does not know how to change, then it is a training problem.

2.  Be willing to change

       If a person does not want to change, then it is a motivation problem.

3.  Not be prevented from changing

       If a person is not allowed to change, then it is a systemic problem.

In all three cases, however, it is also necessary that a person want to change in order for
change to occur. People will want to change when changing will either enable them to obtain
something which they want but don't have - or enable them to avoid something that they
don't want but will get if they don't change.

In other words you can stipulate that people either move towards something or they move
away from something.

Values are the things that people move toward or away from.

Values are those things that a person is willing to invest time, energy, and resources to either
achieve or avoid. Values are the reflection of a fundamental human process. This process is
called valuing or preferring. People prefer or value some people, circumstances and events
over other people, circumstances and events.

So if you want to influence, motivate or change someone, the key to doing so is to connect up
the change you want them to make with the values that they are already operating out of.

The approach to do so is to understand what they want and don't want and connect the
change to either enabling them to get them what they want or to avoid what they don't want.

This may sound easy in this context but it is not. The situation of change differs from the
process of changing people’s values. Some are easier than others. Core values are very difficult
to change.

Values and beliefs and behaviors are all interconnected.

A value tells us what a person wants and doesn’t want.

Beliefs tell us what actions that they think will enable them to get what they want. People may
want the same things but have totally different beliefs about what will cause it to happen.

Understanding value has for most modern Western people and cultures been based on the
definition in Maslow's theories of psychological behavior and existence. Abraham Harold
Maslow (1908-1970) was an American psychologist and the founder of Humanistic Psychology.

Maslow introduced in "A Theory of Metamotivation - The Biological Rooting of Value Life" his
now famous and well-known "Pyramid of Needs": From pure fundamental needs for food and
survival in five steps to the upper level of true self realization.

Most people accept this as a theory for the whole world - the modern industrial society - as
well as the so-called undeveloped 3rd world and the East. In these regions, however, other
fundamental and often both religious and shaman-like cultures and values exists. Even though
you might say that the upper level of these partly "mystical" values and beliefs in essence is to
transform into a higher self, and thereby is very similar to Maslow's upper level in his theory,
different environments, milieu and physical surroundings makes the difference.

Abraham Maslow has, however, had a profound impact on the contemporary understanding of
values. Maslow argued that man's values are determined by his needs, and that as man's
needs change his values change.

Maslow identified five levels of human needs. He believed that each lower need had to be
fulfilled before a person could go on to the next higher need level.

The five levels are:

1. Basic needs. This includes psychological or survival needs for food, sex, clothing, shelter and
   physical fitness.

2. Safety needs. These include health care, fringe benefits, routine, stability, financial reward,
   safety, and security.

3. Love, affection and belongingness needs. This includes social needs for affection and caring
   relationships, trust, feedback, friendship, discussions, being informed, and helping other
   people.

4. Esteem or ego needs. These include esteem needs for accomplishment, participation,
   prestige, and self-esteem, independent thought and action, privileges, authority,
   recognition, and professional group memberships.

5. Self-actualization needs. This includes the inherent needs for well-being, self-fulfillment,
   personal growth, development, and the opportunity to fulfill one's basic potential and to
  become more like one's natural self.

Based on the works of Maslow another psychologist and scientist used almost 30 years of

This scientist - Professor Clare W. Graves (1914-1986) - was a contemporary of Maslow. He set
out to study and verify Maslow's model. He found that Maslow's model failed to characterize
many people, and in time he was forced to abandon it and create a new model of his own of

characterize these other views of self actualization and to show their relationship to Maslow
and to each other.

He worked with this as an empirical study through his whole career. He did not publish much
and have not achieved the same "fame" as Maslow. As a matter of fact Graves was thought of
as a grumpy old psychology professor and most of his students and colleagues remembered

Each value system reflects how people think (i.e. their values or beliefs structures) as opposed
to what people think (i.e. the value or belief content). Value systems apply to schemas or ways
of thinking and not to themes or the particular content of a way of thinking. Often values
appear to be diametrically opposed such as fundamentalist religion and so called "Godless
atheistic communism" who really share the same value system.

Value systems emerge as coping mechanisms in response to problems. As the problems that a
person is dealing with change, then they must develop new value systems in order to continue
to cope. Value systems are evaluated in terms of relevance to the problems that they are
addressing. They are appropriate when they are congruent with the problems being
confronted. It is not appropriate to compare and contrast them with each other independent
of context. Value systems represent the deep structure of coping and preferring and as such
are only revelatory with respect to the choices available. Each level is a coping system in
response to a class of problems.

Graves emphasizes that the deep structural patterns elaborated by his model represents ways
of thinking - not types of people. The deep structure patterns chart bio-psychological systems
and are neither types of people nor discrete stages in the development of a person. Instead
they reflect systems within a person’s repertoire of responses. Each person possesses certain
potential mind sets that can be engaged by different contexts or situations.
