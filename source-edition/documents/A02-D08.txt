   What Must Art Become to Change Artificial Intelligence?

            A ‘more-than-human’ netnography of AI art
                                                                                                                                                                   -
Research inquiry
During the year 2021, a new breed of image-making bots joined social media. Emerging from
online communities for research on artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML),
these bots can answer people’s textual requests with a unique image. So, if I ask a bot for a
photograph of Hito Steyerl as painted on a canvas, I will receive exactly that:

This image was made without any template from the real world. I created it with the program
VQGAN-CLIP that utilises deep learning and neural networks to ‘paint through words’. As an
art historical event, my image should be dismissed as a photorealistic variation of a calligram.
However, as a turn in the history of images and visual culture, it takes part in what the artist
Gregory Chatonsky (2021a) has conceptualised as the last images, as an image of all images
where “in each generated image there is an infinity of other underlying images and texts that
have fed its production”. This is a genre of image-making where everything can be visualised
as a statistical amplification of tendencies in the cultural archives.

The project’s working hypothesis is that new ‘AI art’ provides a view into AI’s sociotechnical
system. I find AI images to crystallise that a complex of “technical artefacts, human agents,
institutions and artificial agents can be said to embody certain values” (Poel 2020). My image
analysis will focus on how fx. bigotry or kitsch (always there in culture, and by that in AI) is
systematically constructed by the actions of bots, data, tech firms, web design, myself, etc.
My main research question is how the creation of AI images can affect the whole system.

Conducting a netnographic study within AI research chatrooms, I will engage, observe, and
program image-making bots in a ‘more-than-human’ culture (Lugosi & Quinton 2018) where
machines are participants in creation, conversation and play. I focus on images produced with
a method of text-to-image generation that, in the last few years, has moved from the margins
of AI research (Reed et al. 2016; Ramesh et al. 2021) into a community-based art movement
called neural art (Audry 2021). My primary case study is on how this technique is used in the
communities to form collective and profitable projects for building an autonomous AI artist,
e.g. quasi-evangelical Abraham (Kogan 2019) and transhumanist Botto (Klingemann 2021).

State of the art
The project contributes to a new, critical line of research on AI and art in a cultural context. It
has two strands: 1) a sociopolitical critique from AI artistic researchers (Crawford & Paglen
2019; Steyerl 2017) and 2) a sociotechnical from academia (Tromble 2020; Zylinska 2020).

In this application, I prioritise the technical to reframe the cultural discussion on AI and art. I
focus on a problem of “what art can do for AI” (Tromble 2020) and move beyond disruptions
of ‘will AI artists change art?’ (Rtology 2021). This is primarily a sociotechnical issue, as an
exclusively political critique of e.g. bias can obscure greater visions of how AI can synthesise
cultural data (Chatonsky 2021b) and create new technical platforms for the cognitive workers
of the world (Berardi 2017).

Specifically, my project responds to a call from one hundred Stanford researchers that asks
for ‘deep interdisciplinary collaborations’ to handle the ‘fundamentally sociotechnical nature’
of large-scale ML models (Bommasani et al. 2021). However, despite addressing image and
text generation models CLIP (Radford et al. 2021), DALL-E (Ramesh et al. 2021) and GPT-3
(Brown et al. 2020) which are widely used to create AI art, the 160 pages report fail to name
the words artist or author, while artistic is used only once to describe legal cases of creative
copyright. This omission illustrates how AI researchers regard art more as product of than a
method for AI, and they thereby inhibit the interdisciplinary critique.

These lines of critical inquiry are relative newcomers to research on AI art, as its pioneering
artists often raised philosophical issues such as can AI really make art? or can machines truly
be creative? (Cohen 1995; Colton 2012). Gradually, this high, theoretical attitude phased out,
because it was deemed to obstruct a concrete analysis of the products of computer art (Boden
2007). Now, scholars argue that other, broader issues arise with AI art that makes it necessary
to ‘not remain just in the realm of aesthetics’ (Zylinska 2020).

This is the case in my analysis of Abraham (Kogan 2019) and Botto (Klingemann 2021) who
are projects for making neural art autonomous through blockchain technology. I problematise
how the notions of agency and autonomy (Kogan 2019) are employed in a concept of art that
is defined by the collective intelligence of a community with an incentive for profit:

- Botto (2021): Depression Trip. The ‘Bottonians’ voted for this as art and sold it as an NFT.

Theory
As mentioned, my thesis is that one can view the sociotechnical system of AI with its images.
This ascribes a sort of agency to AI images. I will: 1) discuss how machine vision is concept-
ualised in (post)humanities literature, 2) sketch a visual culture framework for AI images that
includes the anthropology of art and technics’ thinking on agency in sociotechnical objects.

Centrally, I address a conflict within AI art on how to distinguish ‘agency’ from ‘autonomy’.
E.g., the maker of Abraham defines agency in the collective intelligence of a creator whose
work achieves autonomy by being on a market (Kogan 2019). However, this raises a problem
where agency is extended from human engineers to AI, but not from the AI to its images, as
images cannot ‘create’ anything themselves.

In the critical literature on machine vision, I will discuss theory that assess agency in images.
Offert and Bell (2020) have developed W. J. T. Mitchell’s concept of the metapicture to
theorise AI images as reflections on the limits of machine vision systems. The agency of AI
images is here to act against the intentions of interpretation. Also, Mackenzie and Munster
(2019) have expanded Deleuze and Guattari’s notion of agencement (French for assemblage)
to undermine the autonomy of machine vision with an understanding of technical images as a
multiplicity of actions. These approaches deconstruct the reasonings of Abraham and Botto,
so that agency describes how images affect AI rather than how AI achieves autonomy.

My focus on neural network art poses a new challenge for image and visual culture theory, as
its images differ from digital art or computer-generated imagery by having been created from
scratch by processes of ML. Somaini (2021) has argued that AI images challenge scholars of
visual culture after the ‘iconic turn’ (Boehm 1994) or ‘pictorial turn’ (Mitchell 1992) on how
to develop concepts without “language-based disciplines such as semiotics, when the new
technologies of machine vision are entirely based on a strict interrelation between words and
images”. I find Offert and Bell’s (2020) development of Mitchell’s theory to be important for
this aim, as they manage to view AI through the lens of images.

However, analysing AI images as sociotechnical agents with a real material effect (capable of
changing AI) requires my project to go further than what Mitchell committed to as thought
experiments (2005). I will engage how the anthropology of art and technics (Coupaye 2020)
has related Mitchell’s image theory to how objects are enchanted with a social agency (Gell
1998) and how technicity forms a milieu for specific actions and modalities (Leroi-Gourhan
1945; Simondon 1958). As such, I can analyse how AI images relate to community cultures
and more generally to technical systems. I will do this as a digital ethnography that describes
the formations of sociotechnical agency in AI’s material design and function.

Methodology
My study is netnographic, as all the analysed images are produced, circulated and discussed
in online chatrooms and social media such as Discord, Github, Medium, Slack and Twitter.
My main resources will be the Discord-channels for Abraham, Botto and hacklab EleutherAI,
which all bring together a wide range of AI art practitioners. The sites are publicly accessible
and referenceable. Discord is an important case for doing ‘more-than-human’ netnography to

engage fx. algorithms and bots in the social space of online communities (Lugosi & Quinton
2018), because Discord has a programming interface for bot development. The bots take part
in conversations, organise content and offer access to AI art experiments. I will research from
the perspective of both AI and myself how images form a sociality between bots and humans.

My approach is more multimodal than classical text-based netnography but follows the same
qualitative methodology of participant-observation (Kozinets 2019). I will create researcher
profiles for the sites and collect, curate and analyse their archives of discussions, images and
artistic-technical developments. Gathering fieldwork data, I will conduct most interactions
with the communities through bots, so that I will have automatic research profiles that deploy
text generation software, while many of my comments will be multimodal (code-images-text)
requests for text-to-image generation bots. Using intermediary agents will make it possible to
include the communities in difficult discussions about issues of agency for AI and images, as
my inquiry will be similar to other activities in the culture and thus be subject to criticism.

A central methodological focus is on how the technical system distinguishes images from art.
I will draw upon Gillian Rose’s concepts for the sites of production and circulation of images
(Rose 2016) to analyse how the images of Abraham and Botto count as art when circulating
at commercial markets, while all images produced at EleutherAI are already considered to be
art. As these images are produced with the same tools, I will follow how some turn into art in
their circulation by use of simple web scraping and Discord’s search function.
