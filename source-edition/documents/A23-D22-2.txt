AUTOMATIC UPRISINGSARCHIVING THE SYNTHETIC INTERNATIONAL“The bureaucratic mentality is the only constant in the universe”Dr. Leonard McCoy in Star Trek IV: The Voyage Home (1986)Background and purposeThis project raises a double-edged scenario: what to do if the “robot uprising” has already happened, and the prophesied explosion of artificial superintelligence only led to an endless supercharging of bureaucratic stupidity? 1) Should this turn buzzwords of artificial intelligence (AI), disruption and digitisation into an antagonist democratic threat on a par with how global social movements fight capitalism, climate change, and racial injustice?2) Or could one imagine that bureaucratic robot power is a chance to ally with the machines with the aim of causing a genuine democratic revolt?Given that any research project should fight stupidity, the radical question becomes: should a societal robot uprising evoke societal protest - or a revolution? That is: Can today’s society actually change technology, or should people and technology first set out to remake society?The purpose of the practice-based project will be to research a vision for our robot uprising by making an image archive for a potential organization that acts as its representative. The idea is to document a quasi-fictitious organization, The Synthetic International (SI), as an advocacy group driven by a machine learning model. From 2023-26, I will thus collect visual data from real AI advocacies, and rework source materials (chatbot transcripts, interviews, images) that I evoked and facilitated through the global media story of The Synthetic Party. The Synthetic International’s website will have AI text-to-image models as its public frontend that can respond to textual questions with e.g. diagrams or photos of current robot uprisings.The output will be 1) three digital art exhibitions curated by Aksioma (Slovenia), Kunsthal Aarhus (Denmark) and POINT (Cyprus), and 2) three journal articles to form a written thesis in AI & Society: “An Iconology of AI Advocacies: Protest or Revolution?“, Études Digitales: “Contributory, Practice-Based Research in the wake of La Société Automatique”, and e-flux: “Automatic Uprisings: Lessons from Archiving a Synthetic International”.The project’s objective is that the SI presents the radical dilemma of “protest or revolution” as a viable ethical problem that contributes to the recent surge of scholarship on democracy and AI in Aarhus University at the SHAPE research centre, and nationwide through the ADD project (Algorithms, Data and Democracy).Generally, the SI image archive is an impossible synthesis of AI advocacy positions. I will collect new data that provides a hodgepodge of 1) artworks and photos on AI’s harms and social implications from the materials of AI-bias advocacy groups (Ada Lovelace Institute, Algorithmic Justice League, AlgorithmWatch, etc.), and 2) diagrams and graphs on AI’s existential risks as developed by AI-safety advocacy groups (Centre for Study of Existential Risk, MIRI, Future of Humanity Institute, etc.). The aim of such a synthetic mixture of the “left and right-wing” of AI advocacy (Eliot 2022) is to inject a socio-emancipatory sensibility into the ultra-rationalist imaginary surrounding the AI takeover.HypothesisThe project’s main hypothesis is that contributory, practice-based research can develop an immanent critique of the power that AI already exercises within the bureaucracy and public spheres in a way that reaches beyond how critical theory dismisses democratic uses for AI. The point of this theoretical transgression is how the archive can enact potentialities. The objective is to research the current impact of AI’s synthetic images on the imagination of our democratic society by collecting and enabling new visions for protest and revolution with AI.Recent important works of critical theory on AI have argued against democratization (active participation and collective ownership), as they evaluate that the data-driven infrastructures of AI inevitably foster a centralization of control and authoritarian forms of power (Crawford 2021; McQuillan 2022). However, critical theory here targets AI from an external perspective whereas my project will employ generative AI as a form of auto-criticism (Chatonsky 2022). By constructing the SI as a potential organization through the process of archiving, the project asserts an ambition to use AI to evoke non-authoritarian imaginaries for this epoch.The project will here develop upon a philosophical conception of the archive as a peculiar temporal object that enacts multiple potentialities. The vital thing to note is how contributory research in digital studies (see Methodology) is based upon an interpretation of the Husserl- ian theory of retentions and protentions (Stiegler 2014). Philosopher Yuk Hui has developed this interpretation through the concept of tertiary protention as a “new synthesis of time” by analyzing the archive in an age of big data and algorithms - the two constituents of AI (Hui 2018). Simply put: Hui posits that archiving is now not only a way of conserving the past to anticipate the future, but a tool of prediction that makes up the future through the past. What this theory of potentiality brings to the proposed project is to allow for its political imaginaries on AI to manufacture the empirical evidence, e.g.. by generating data.MethodologyThe project’s methodologies can be summarized as an “archivism” by which I update a long tradition of avant-garde art for relating bureaucratic pastiche with new media technologies (Spieker 2008). The project will deploy: 1) experimental methods from creative AI practices such as data collection, machine learning training, and prompt engineering, then contributory research frameworks such as workshops and online facilitation, 2) artistic-visual techniques, e.g. collages and montages, web design, and remediations between analogue-digital media, and 3) theoretical methods such as conceptual critique and iconological image analysis.The thesis will orient this experimental apparatus within a digital studies’ philosophy of technology to interpret the SI image archive in relation to AI’s ideological superstructure (as theorized by e.g. Bernard Stiegler, Yuk Hui & Joanna Zylinska). To work out this link between visual material and techno-political ideology, it turns to contextual methods of image analysis in the tradition of Aby Warburg and Erwin Panofsky’s iconological syntheses (Mitchell 1986).A bridging of creative AI with contributory research constitutes the methodological innovation of this project in comparison to the few practice-based research projects on art and current AI that are already published. While their concern is how AI affects artistic practice (Amerika 2022), this project rather asks how practice changes AI (Tromble 2020). Contributory resear- ch can attempt this by facilitating others' work. In the context of artistic practice, contribution is the digital studies’ interpretation of socially-engaged art as a challenge to algorithmic gov- ernmentality, as it inscribes multiplicities in the hermeneutics of a creative research practice (Stiegler 2021). My take is mostly workshops and facilitating human-AI interactions online.[████]
