             AUTOMATIC UPRISINGS

                ARCHIVING A TECHNO-SOCIAL SCULPTURE


      “The bureaucratic mentality is the only constant in the universe”

     -   Dr. Leonard McCoy in Star Trek IV: The Voyage Home (1986)


Background and purpose

AUTOMATIC UPRISINGS raises a double-edged scenario: what to do if the “robot uprising”

has already happened, and the prophesied explosion of artificial superintelligence only led to

an endless supercharging of bureaucratic stupidity?


       1) Should this turn buzzwords of artificial intelligence (AI), disruption and digitisation

        into an antagonist democratic threat on a par with how global social movements fight

        capitalism, climate change, and racial injustice?

       2) Or could one imagine that bureaucratic robot power is a chance to ally with the

      machines with the aim of causing genuine democratic revolts?


Given that one wants to fight stupidity, the radical question becomes: should a societal robot

uprising evoke societal protest - or a revolution? That is: Can today’s society actually change

technology, or should people and technology first set out to remake society?


The purpose of the practice-based project will be to research a vision for our robot uprising

by making an image archive for a potential organization that acts as its representative. The

idea is thus to document a “techno-social sculpture”, The Synthetic International (SI), as an

advocacy group driven by a machine learning model. From 2023-26, I will collect visual data

from human-led AI advocacies, and rework source materials (chatbot transcripts, interviews,

images) that I evoked and facilitated through the global media artwork The Synthetic Party.

The Synthetic International’s website will have AI text-to-image models as its public frontend

that can respond to textual questions with e.g. diagrams or photos of current robot uprisings.


The output will be 1) a laboratory on socially-engaged art with AI as techno-social sculpting

in Kunsthal Aarhus, 2) three digital art exhibitions curated by Aksioma (Slovenia), Kunsthal

Aarhus (Denmark) and POINT (Cyprus), and 3) three journal articles to form a written thesis

in AI & Society: “An Iconology of AI Advocacies: Protest or Revolution?“, Études Digitales:

“Imagining a Techno- Social Sculpture in the wake of La Société Automatique”, and e-flux:

“Automatic Uprisings: Lessons from Archiving a Synthetic International”.

The project’s objective is that the SI presents the radical dilemma of “protest or revolution”

as a viable ethical problem that contributes to the recent surge of scholarship on democracy

and AI in Aarhus University at the SHAPE research centre, and nationwide through the ADD

project (Algorithms, Data and Democracy). The means to this is a reimagining of socially-

engaged art as a structural challenge to current algorithmic governmentality (Stiegler 2021).


Generally, the SI image archive is an impossible synthesis of AI advocacy positions. I will

collect new data that provides a hodgepodge of 1) artworks and photos on AI’s harms and

social implications from the materials of AI-bias advocacy groups (Ada Lovelace Institute,

Algorithmic Justice League, AlgorithmWatch, etc.), and 2) diagrams and graphs on AI’s

existential risks as developed by AI-safety advocacy groups (Centre for Study of Existential

Risk, MIRI, Future of Humanity Institute, etc.). The aim of such a synthetic mixture of the “left

and right-wing” of AI advocacy (Eliot 2022) is to inject a socio-emancipatory sensibility into

the ultra-rationalist imaginary surrounding the AI takeover.


Hypothesis

The project’s main hypothesis is that contributory, practice-based research can develop an

immanent critique of the power that AI already exercises within the bureaucracy and public

spheres in a way that reaches beyond how critical theory dismisses democratic uses for AI.

The point of this theoretical transgression is how the archive can enact potentialities. The

objective is to research the current impact of AI’s synthetic images on the imagination of our

democratic society by collecting and enabling new visions for protest and revolution with AI.


Recent important works of critical theory on AI have argued against democratization (active

participation and collective ownership), as they evaluate that the data-driven infrastructures

of AI inevitably foster a centralization of control and authoritarian forms of power (Crawford

2021; McQuillan 2022). However, critical theory here targets AI from an external perspective

whereas my project will employ generative AI as a form of auto-criticism (Chatonsky 2022).


The project will here develop upon a philosophical conception of the archive as a peculiar

temporal object that enacts multiple potentialities. The vital thing to note is how contributory

research in digital studies (see Methodology) is based upon an interpretation of the Husserl-

ian theory of retentions and protentions (Stiegler 2014). Philosopher Yuk Hui has developed

this interpretation through the concept of tertiary protention as a “new synthesis of time” by

analyzing the archive in an age of big data and algorithms - the two constituents of AI (Hui

2018). Simply put: Hui posits that archiving is now not only a way of conserving the past to

Methodology

The project’s methodologies can be summarized as an “archivism” by which I update a long

tradition of avant-garde art for relating bureaucratic pastiche with new media technologies

(Spieker 2008). The project will deploy: 1) experimental methods from creative AI practices

such as data collection, machine learning training, and prompt engineering, plus contributory

research frameworks such as workshops and online facilitation, 2) artistic-visual techniques,

e.g. collages and montages, web design, and remediations between analogue-digital media,

and 3) theoretical methods such as conceptual critique and iconological image analysis.


The thesis  will orient the experimental apparatus within a digital studies’ philosophy of

technology to interpret the SI image archive in relation to AI’s ideological superstructure (as

theorized by e.g. Bernard Stiegler, Yuk Hui & Joanna Zylinska). To work out this link between

visual material and techno-political ideology, it turns to contextual methods of image analysis

in the tradition of Aby Warburg and Erwin Panofsky’s iconological syntheses (Mitchell 1986).


A bridging of creative AI with contributory research constitutes the methodological innovation

of this project in comparison to the few practice-based research projects on art and current

AI that are already published. While their concern is how AI affects artistic practice (Amerika

2022), this project rather asks how practice changes AI (Tromble 2020). Contributory resear-

ch can attempt this by facilitating others' work. In the context of artistic practice, contribution

is a digital studies’ interpretation of Beuysian social sculpting as an inscription of multiplicity

(human and non-human) that frames the hermeneutics of creative research (Stiegler 2021).

My takes are open labs, workshops, and facilitating human-AI interactions.
