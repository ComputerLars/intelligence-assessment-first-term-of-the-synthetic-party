             AUTOMATIC UPRISINGS

             ARCHIVING THE SYNTHETIC INTERNATIONAL


      “The bureaucratic mentality is the only constant in the universe”

     -   Dr. Leonard McCoy in Star Trek IV: The Voyage Home (1987)


Background and purpose

This project raises a double-edged scenario: what to do  if the “robot uprising” has already

happened, and the prophesied explosion of artificial superintelligence only led to an endless

supercharging of bureaucratic stupidity?


       1) Should this turn the buzzwords of artificial intelligence, disruption and digitisation

        into an antagonist democratic threat on a par with how global social movements fight

        capitalism, climate change, and racial injustice?

       2) Or could one imagine that bureaucratic robot power is a chance to ally with the

      machines with the aim of causing a genuine democratic revolt?


Given that any research project should fight stupidity, the ethical dilemma becomes: should

this robot uprising evoke societal protest - or a revolution? That is: Can today’s society

actually change technology, or should people and technology first set out to remake society?


The purpose of the research project will be to research a vision for a societal robot uprising

by making an image archive for a potential organization that acts as its representative. The

idea is to document a quasi-fictitious organization, The Synthetic International (SI), as an

advocacy group driven by artificial intelligence (AI). From 2023-26, I will thus collect visual

data from real AI advocacies, and rework source materials (chatbot transcripts, interviews,

images) that I evoked and facilitated through the global media story of The Synthetic Party.

The Synthetic International’s website will have AI text-to-image model’s as its public frontend

that will respond to textual questions with e. g. diagrams or photos of current robot uprisings.


The project’s objective is to frame the SI through the dilemma of “protest or revolution” as a

viable ethical problem that can contribute to the recent surge of scholarship on democracy

and AI. This is facilitated in Aarhus at the SHAPE research centre, and nationwide through

the ADD project (Algorithms, Data and Democracy).

The SI archive is an impossible synthesis of AI advocacy positions. I will collect new data

that provides a hodgepodge of 1) artworks and images on AI’s harms and social implications

from the materials of AI-bias advocacy groups (Ada Lovelace Institute, Algorithmic Justice

League, AlgorithmWatch, etc.), and 2) diagrams and graphs on AI’s existential risks as

developed by AI-safety advocacy groups (Centre for Study of Existential Risk, MIRI, Future

of Humanity Institute, etc.). The aim of such a synthetic mixture of the “left and right-wing” of

AI advocacies is to inject a socio-emancipatory sensibility into the ultra-rationalist imaginary

surrounding the AI takeover.


The project’s ambition is to produce new artworks in genres of bureaucracy, schemes, and

propaganda that can serve as materials to be analyzed by the written thesis as visions of

“automatic uprisings”. Importantly, employing AI to archive the SI clarifies that I do not posit

sentient AI in any present tense. Also, as an “archivism” I do not seek out active mobilization

but rather attempts to imagine new forms of participation.


Hypothesis and state of the art

The project’s main hypothesis is that practice-based research can develop an immanent

critique of the power that AI already exercises within bureaucracy and public spheres in a

way that reaches beyond how critical theory dismisses any actual democratic use for AI. The

point of this theoretical transgression is how the archive can enact potentialities, which I will

realize through a framework for contributory research practices in the field of digital studies.

The thesis’ objective is to target the current impact of AI and synthetic images on democratic

societies by outlining visions for both protest and revolution with AI.


State of the art

Recent important works of critical theory on AI have argued against democratization (active

participation and collective ownership), as they recognize that the data-driven infrastructures

of AI inevitably foster a centralization of control and authoritarian forms of power (Crawford

2021; McQuillan 2022). However, critical theory here targets AI from an external perspective

whereas my project will employ generative AI as a form of criticism in itself (see Chatonsky

2022 for a distinction on the academic and artistic critiques of AI). By constructing the SI as

a potential organization through the process of archiving, I assert the ambition to use AI for

evoking non-authoritarian imaginaries for our present era.


The project will here develop upon a philosophical conception of the archive as a peculiar

temporal object that enacts multiple potentialities. The vital thing to note is how contributory,

practice-based research in digital studies is based upon an interpretation of the Husserlian

theory of retentions and protentions (Stiegler 2014). The philosopher Yuk Hui has developed

this interpretation through the concept of tertiary protention as a “new synthesis of time” by

analyzing the archive in an age of big data and algorithms - the two constituents of AI (Hui

2018). Simply put: Hui posits that archiving is now not only a way of conserving the past to

anticipate the future, but a tool of prediction that makes up the future through the past. What

this theory of potentiality brings to my project is that it allows for its political imaginaries of AI

to go beyond the empirical evidence.


Finally, a practice-based project can make up for an omission in the politics of both digital

studies and relevant artistic and critical projects for change (e.g. Staal 2021; Muldoon 2022;

Tarnoff 2022) as they are all only targeting our existing technologies. But what if the problem

is firstly embedded in the logic of all the applications of data infrastructures? This project’s

aim is that a visual synthesis of contradictory positions (as derived from AI advocacies) can

offer a non-existent imaginary (Zylinska 2020) for a new future of what I propose to theorize

conceptually as “automatic uprisings”.


The thesis’ main delivery  will be the publication of three articles to form the bulk of a

dissertation. They will respectively analyze the project’s data, methods, and message: 1) AI

& Society: “An Iconology of AI Advocacies: Protest or Revolution?“, 2) Études Digitales:

“Doing Contributory, Practice-Based Research in the wake of La Société Automatique”, & 3)

e-flux: “Automatic Uprisings: Lessons from Archiving a Synthetic International”.


Methodology

The project’s methodologies can be summarized as an “archivism” by which I update a long

tradition of avant-garde art for relating bureaucratic pastiche with new media technologies

(Spieker 2008). The project will deploy 1) experimental methods from creative AI practices

such as data collection, machine learning training, and prompt engineering, and contributory

research frameworks such as workshops, and 2) conventional artistic-visual techniques, e.g.

collages, web design, and remediations of analogue and digital media. The 3) theoretical

methods are mostly conceptual and visual analysis.


The thesis  will orient the experimental apparatus within a digital studies philosophy of

technology to interpret the image archive in relation to AI’s ideological superstructures (as it

has been theorized by e. g. Bernard Stiegler, Yuk Hui and Joanna Zylinska). To work out the

link between visual materials and techno-political ideology, I will go for contextual methods of

image analysis in the tradition of Aby Warburg and Erwin Panofsky’s iconological syntheses

(Mitchell 1986).

The bridging of creative AI with contributory research constitutes the central methodological

innovation of this project in comparison to the relatively few practice-based research projects

on art and machine learning that have been published. While their concern is how AI affects

artistic practice (Amerika 2022), my project rather asks how art can change AI (Tromble

2020). The contributory form is not to rally people but to allow for the work of others as a part

in the hermeneutics of a creative research practice (Stiegler 2021), which this project will do

with contributory workshops and by collecting facilitated human-AI interactions online.
