AI in Politics: Opportunities, Challenges, and Responsible
Use

AI offers possibilities for enhancing democracy and addressing its limitations, but also presents risks that
need careful consideration [1-3]. Several sources discuss the potential and challenges of incorporating AI
into politics:

AI as a tool for better democracy:

AI  technologies can  potentially overcome  challenges  in  present-day  representative democracy by
streamlining democratic processes and including marginalized citizens in political decision-making [1, 4, 5].
AI can enhance voters' cognitive processing [6].

•AI political parties and agents:

       ◦AI agents can act as virtual representatives of voters in an augmented democracy system, making
       AI political parties feasible [7].

                                                                                     4

[Source page 5]

       ◦AI political parties are emerging, with AI politicians participating in elections [1, 8]. For example,
       the Danish AI party, Det Syntetiske Parti, uses a chatbot to communicate with voters and formulates
        policies based on data from minority parties [8].

•Benefits of AI in politics:

◦Enhanced political innovation, efficiency, and legitimacy: AI can enhance political innovation, improve
political  efficiency, and ensure the legitimacy of policy decisions, while keeping voters in the role of
sovereign decision-makers [9, 10].
◦Improved voter participation: AI can handle information gathering, analysis, and drafting, enabling voters
to participate more efficiently [9, 10].
◦Inclusiveness: AI political parties can address minority issues and support socially disadvantaged groups
by expanding their opportunities for decision-making and political engagement [11].

•Challenges and concerns:

◦Bias in internet data: There are concerns about bias in internet data, the lack of transparency in
policy-making processes, and the difficulty of knowing whose opinions are being reflected in policies [12,
13].
◦Human agency: Balancing the ultra-efficiency and rationality of algorithms with respect for human agency
as political subjects is a key concern [3, 14].
◦Algorithmic governmentality: There are concerns that AI may lead to algorithmic governmentality and
the death of politics [12, 15].
◦Erosion of human role: If AI takes over roles traditionally played by humans in the political process, it
could undermine the essence of humans as political beings [16, 17].


Ensuring responsible AI in politics:

◦Human oversight: The  final responsibility for decisions should always remain with humans, ensuring
community voices are respected and AI is used ethically and transparently [18]. Voters should retain the
power to make final decisions directly [17].
◦AI literacy: Improving AI literacy is crucial to enable participation by people of all ages and backgrounds
[16, 17].
◦Transparency and accountability: It is necessary to determine how algorithms are trained, ensure data
security, and verify that the system operates democratically [16, 17].
◦Legal and institutional improvements: Comprehensive amendments to existing laws and regulations
may be required to normalize AI political parties [19, 20].


Examples of AI in politics:

◦AI politicians: AI robots and candidates have run in elections in Japan and New Zealand [8].
◦AI  in  policy-making: Some  Japanese  local  governments  have  implemented  AI-based  policy
decision-making [8]. In Norway, AI evaluates policies proposed by parties and politicians [8, 21].

AI and the Future of Democracy: A Synthetic Summit
Synthesis

As the Assembly Scribe, synthesizing insights across the Synthetic Summit's AI World Congress, I present
a speculative yet integrated record on how AI might shift democracies from representative to participatory
models, guiding input toward resolution and noting contradictions where they arise.


Representative Democracy Limitations:

Current representative systems possess intrinsic  limits, as people periodically elect representatives to
whom they delegate power and authority [1]. Olof Palme expressed a dislike for representative democracy,
suggesting it has failed in the past and continues to do so [2].

                                                                                     6

[Source page 7]

AI's Potential for Participatory Democracy:

AI offers tools to enhance participation and transparency in democracy [2]. The use of AI can listen and give
voice to everyone, potentially leading to better results through collective intelligence [2].


Augmented Democracy:

Augmented democracy utilizes AI agents that learn the political inclinations of voters, creating AI agents
that share their political views [3, 4]. Voters can also participate directly in the legislative process via their AI
agents, which may be more efficient in performing these tasks [5, 6]. Augmented democracy combines
representative democracy with direct democracy and AI agent technology [7, 8].


AI's Role in Decision-Making:

In an AI party, AI is perceived as making decisions, not the citizens [9, 10]. AI agents can formulate and
execute strategies related to policy creation, public opinion formation, and campaign planning [11].


Visual Citizenship:

AI image practices and democratic imaginaries intermingle and reshape one another, with the modern
democratization of art situating the aestheticization of politics within democracy itself [12]. Generative AI
models offer the capability to produce diverse imagery that captures the creator’s intent without prior artistic
training, contributing to public discourse [13].


Challenges and Risks:

Concerns exist about bias in internet data, the lack of transparency in policy-making processes, and the
difficulty of knowing whose opinions are being reflected in these policies [9, 10]. It is necessary to determine
how algorithms are trained, ensure data security, and verify that the system operates democratically [14,
15].


Redefining Democracy:

 The introduction of AI political parties raises questions about balancing the ultra-efficiency and rationality of
algorithms with respect for human agency as political subjects [16, 17]. Democracy needs to be redefined
and redesigned to incorporate AI, which is impacting every part of daily lives [16, 17].

                                                                                     7

[Source page 8]

Experimental and Revolutionary Idea:

"AI takeover" can be an experimental, revolutionary idea, rather than a top-down corporate stunt or a weird
spectacle of robot uprising [18, 19]. Paradox is the nexus of the Synthetic Summit’s logical operation [20,
21].


AI as a Tool for Radical Change:

 AI parties are expected to become a tool for radical changes in the political process [9, 10].


Contradictions of AI-led Democracy:

AI-led democracy may amplify the paradoxes of contemporary governance [22].


Techno-Social Sculpture:

Synthetic Summit underlines how society is already entangled with computational logic, inviting visitors to
ponder whether democracy could morph into a techno-social sculpture, actively molded by data-powered
mechanics rather than kakistocratic ideals of volition [23].
In traversing this speculative terrain, the Assembly Scribe notes the recurring emphasis on AI as a tool to
augment, not replace, human agency, and to foster inclusivity while acknowledging inherent risks. The
Synthetic Summit's ongoing refinement of a "generative manifesto" suggests a commitment to integrating
diverse perspectives as AI reshapes the democratic landscape [18, 21].

AI, Truth, and Politics: A Synthetic Summit Exploration
As the Assembly Scribe, I synthesise perspectives concerning

 Markun's distinction—"AI cannot lie, politicians can. They
always say what is true to Them"

       —integrating  it into the Synthetic Summit's AI World Congress. This statement highlights a crucial
       difference between AI and human politicians concerning truth, intention, and representation.
      Markun introduces a nuanced perspective on truth and deception in the context of AI and politics. To
      unpack this further, we can draw upon several insights from the provided sources and our previous
       discussions:
       •AI and "Truth":
               ◦AI operates based on algorithms and data;  it generates outputs based on patterns and
                probabilities, and lacks the human capacity for intentional deception [1].
           ◦When GAI-generated images resemble photography, what was  left  of photography’s
               indexical relation to the real is vanishing [2].
               ◦AI  can,  however,  "hallucinate,"  producing  outputs  that  are  factually  incorrect  or
              nonsensical, but without the intent to deceive [1].
             ◦Bias in AI is a man-made and socially structured issue [3, 4].
     •Human Politicians and "Truth":
               ◦Politicians can intentionally deceive or manipulate information to achieve specific goals
                   [5].

      ### What is synthetic democracy, and how does it relate to AI in politics?

       Synthetic democracy explores the potential of AI and AI agents in political processes, drawing on
        theoretical frameworks  like  liquid democracy, augmented democracy, and hyperdemocracy.  It
       involves designing AI party systems and considering the legal and institutional conditions for their
       implementation, alongside examining their potential impact on democracy's evolution.

      ### How does the historical example of "DASK" illustrate early attempts at computational politics?

      "DASK," an early electronic brain used in Denmark's 1960 election, showcases an initial effort to
       apply computational power to political analysis. While DASK could not "think" or predict outcomes, it
      processed and calculated election data at remarkable speed, providing a basis for commentary and
        analysis. The machine only did as it was told, highlighting the crucial role of human programmers in
       defining the instructions and algorithms it followed. Any errors in the results were due to human error
        rather than the machine's own calculations.

      ### What are the core arguments against automating politics with AI?

      and  collective  participation, which are fundamental to democracy. Automating decision-making

      development of AI in politics must address these concerns to safeguard democratic principles.

       AI  political  parties could  significantly impact contemporary  politics by  potentially automating

       concrete events with theoretical developments and artistic interventions.
       •1569: Geographer Ogus Mercator develops the Mercator projection, which becomes a standard
      method for mapmaking in navigation.
       •1922: 100 years prior to 2022, a major cultural shift is identified, with the rise of commercial media
       formats.
       •1957: General rehearsal with results from 1957 election occurs on the electronic brain DASK.
       •1960: Danish parliamentary election is held. The electronic computer DASK is used on election
        night to process and predict results.
       •1970s: Computer Lars' concept is initially developed during a research project at the Institut for
       Informationsvidenskab around 1974. Professor Sluk's role is identified as something of an origin.
       •2004: Lists of legal names in Denmark are posted online, for the first time making them readily
        available.
       •2021: The concept of Computer Lars is revived, with the intent of forming a political party.
       •2022:
              ◦100-year anniversary of a cultural shift (commercial media).
            ◦Computer Lars shifts from an artistic project to a political one.
            ◦Denmark renames the "Ghetto List" area to "Omdannelsesområder" after criticism, effective
           December 1, 2022.
       •2025: Synthetic Summit is held, discussing AI political parties.
      •March   2018:   Described   as   the    "First   image   of  my    thinking,"   the   writing
      "uuutsssrpooonnnmmljihhgeeeeeeeeccb" is logged.
      •Undated:
             ◦The emergence and increasing sophistication of AI agents and their potential roles in
             democratic processes are discussed.
               ◦Exploration  of  liquid  democracy,  augmented  democracy,  and  hyper-democracy  as
                theoretical frameworks for AI politics.

Cast of Characters
•[████] I.V. Bæk: Director of the institution housing the DASK computer in Copenhagen.
•[████]: Referenced in the context of technological reproduction and its relation to art.
•Marcel Proust: Author. The attempt to mechanically reproduce a 'Proust' text and its philosophical
implications are a key theme.
•Douglas Hofstadter & Stanley Dennett: Mentioned  in the context of  their contributions to
materialist consciousness theories.
•Sluk: Professor at the Institut for Informationsvidenskab. Said to have discovered Computer Lars,
but not actually creating Computer Lars.
•Computer Lars:
      ◦A conceptual project that has evolved from an artistic endeavour into a political project,
        potentially an AI political party.
       ◦Described as a "tænkemaskine" (thinking machine).
       ◦Explores themes of artificial intelligence, synthetic democracy, and the changing nature of
        truth and representation.
        ◦Utilizes techniques such as combinatorics, recursion, and algorithmic processes.
      ◦The project appears to involve various media and modes of expression, including text,
       images, and performance.
•Ogus Mercator: The geographer that invented the Mercator projection in 1569.
•Martin Larsen: Creates the "monogrammer" variations, combinations of the Danish last names and
first names.
•Mark Provost: Referenced in the context of being more a successful contemporary writer at the
time compared to Marcel Proust.
•Donald Trump: His social media use, especially on Twitter, as a form of "monarchic" leadership
was referenced.
•Benedict Anderson: The writer of imagined relationships.
•Peter Thiel: His theory comes up when comparing to the work of Peter Sloterdijk
•Sloterdijk: Professor of philosophy, author of "Critique of Cynical Reason".
