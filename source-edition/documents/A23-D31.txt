AUTOMATIC UPRISINGSARCHIVING A SYNTHETIC INTERNATIONAL          “The bureaucratic mentality is the only constant in the universe”Dr. Leonard McCoy in Star Trek IV: The Voyage Home (1986)Background and purposeThis project raises a double-edged question: what to do if the “robot uprising” already happened, and the prophesied explosion of superintelligence only led to an endless supercharging of bureaucratic stupidity? 1) Should this turn the buzzwords of artificial intelligence, disruption and digitisation into an antagonist democratic threat that is on a par with how global social movements fight against capitalism, climate change, and racial injustice?, or 2) Could one imagine that bureaucratic robot power is the chance to ally with the machines with the aim of causing a real democratic revolt? Given that we want to fight stupidity, the ethical dilemma becomes: should the robot uprising evoke societal protest - or a revolution? That is: Can today’s society actually change technology, or should people and technology first set out to change society?From 2023-26, I propose to research the image of a purely societal robot uprising by collecting new data, and reworking source material (AI images, chatbot transcripts, media coverage) that I evoked with the project The Synthetic Party (“past research”). The purpose of a research project will be to expand these materials into a full image archive for a potential organization that acts as representative for the robot uprising. The artistic idea is to document the work of a potential advocacy organization driven by artificial intelligence (AI). This is named a Synthetic International (SI).As an “archivism”, the methodological ambition is not to engage in any further acts of mobilization or organizing, but is to produce new artworks in genres of bureaucracy, schemes, and propaganda that will serve as materials to be analyzed by the written thesis as examples of “automatic uprisings”. The thesis’ discussion of a SI poses the dilemma of “protest or revolution” as a viable ethical problem that contributes to the recent surge of scholarship on AI and democracy that is facilitated in Aarhus at the SHAPE research center, and nationwide at ADD (Algorithms, Data and Democracy).The SI archive is an impossible synthesis of AI advocacy positions. I will collect new data that provides a hodgepodge of 1) artworks and images on AI’s harms and social implications from the materials of AI-bias advocacy groups (Ada Lovelace Institute, Algorithmic Justice League, AlgorithmWatch, etc.), & 2) diagrams and graphs on AI’s existential risks as developed by AI-safety advocacy groups (Centre for Study of Existential Risk, MIRI, Future of Humanity Institute, etc.). The aim of such a synthetic mixture of the “left and right-wing” of AI advocacies is to inject a socio-emancipatory sensibility into the ultra-rationalist imaginary surrounding the AI takeover. Importantly, using AI to archive a SI clarifies that I do not posit a sentient AI in the present tense.Hypothesis and state of the artThe project’s main hypothesis is that practice-based research can offer an immanent critique of the power that AI already exercises within bureaucracy and public spheres that reaches beyond the way that critical theory dismisses any democratization of AI. The key to this transgression is how the archive can enact potentialities, which I will realize through a framework for contributory, practice-based research as developed by the field of digital studies. The thesis objective is to discuss the political impact of synthetic images in democracy, which it will do by outlining concrete yet non-existent visions for how an automatic uprising could appear in terms of protest and revolution.Recent important works of critical theory on AI have argued against democratization, as they recognize that the data-driven infrastructures of AI foster a centralization of control and authoritarian forms of power (Crawford 2021; McQuillan 2022). However, the critical theorists have analyzed AI from an external perspective, which is different from my project which will employ generative AI as a form of criticism in itself (see Chatonsky 2022 for a similar distinction on the academic and artistic critique of AI). Also, my hypothesis points further as it researches a vision of AI for societal change.By constructing the SI as a potential organization through the process of archiving, I assert the ambition to use AI for evoking non-authoritarian imaginaries of our present era in a way that subsequently could inspire democratic forms of governance with AI.The project here draws upon a digital studies conception of the archive as a peculiar temporal object that enacts potentialities. The vital thing to note is how contributory, practice-based research in digital studies is based upon an interpretation of the Hus- serlian theory of retentions and protentions (Stiegler 2014). The philosopher Yuk Hui has developed this through the concept of tertiary protention as a “new synthesis of time” (Hui 2018) by analyzing the archive in an age of big data and algorithms (the two constituents of AI). Simply put: Hui posits that archiving is now not only a way of conserving the past to anticipate the future, but a tool of prediction that makes up the future through the past. What this theory of potentiality brings to the proposed project is that it allows for its political imaginaries of AI to go beyond the empirical evidence.Finally, a practice-based project can make up for an omission in the politics of both digital studies and relevant artistic and critical projects for change (e.g. Staal 2021; Muldoon 2022; Tarnoff 2022) as they are all only targeting our existing technologies. But what if the problem is firstly embedded in the logic of all the applications of data infrastructures? This project’s aim is that a visual synthesis of contradictory positions (as derived from AI advocacies) can offer a non-existent imaginary (Zylinska 2020) for a new future for what I propose to theorize conceptually as “automatic uprisings”.The thesis’ main deliveries will be the publication of three articles: 1) AI & Society: “An Amalgam of AI Advocacy: Protest or Revolution?“, 2) Études Digitales: “Doing Contributory and Practice-Based Research after La Société Automatique”, and 3) e-flux: “Automatic Uprisings: Lessons from Archiving a Synthetic International”.Methodology and realization of artworkThe project’s methodologies can be summarized as an “archivism” by which I update a long tradition of avant-garde art for relating bureaucratic pastiche with new media technologies (Spieker 2008). The project uses experimental methods from creative AI and contributory research practices such as collaborative workshops, web design, data collection and sorting, training of machine learning models, prompt engineering, and different forms of artistic remediation between analogue and digital technologies. The thesis will orient the experimental apparatus within a digital studies’ philosophy of technology to pose AI’s abyssal context of ethical, political, and social dilemmas.The bridging of creative AI with contributory research constitutes the methodological innovation of this project in comparison to the few practice-based research projects on art and machine learning that have been published. While their concern is what AI does to artistic practice (Amerika 2022), my project also asks how art can change AI (e.g. Tromble 2020). The contributory part is not to rally people, but to advocate - that is to research causes for change in context of concrete institutional challenges (Stiegler 2021), which this project does through contributory workshops (see below).[████]
