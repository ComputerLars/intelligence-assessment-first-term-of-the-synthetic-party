Distinguished delegates, curious onlookers, friends of the improbable, and idle wanderers from around and beyond—welcome.

We gather under the banner of the Synthetic Summit, an odd little place in the swirling cosmos of political conferences and congresses, overshadowed perhaps by so-called Global AI Safety Summits in Bletchley Park, Seoul and Paris, the AI for Good gatherings in the United Nations, and the recurring DOGE-themed fiasco across the Atlantic (we’ve all heard whispers of an “AI-powered government takeover” stunt by Elon {Sieg} Musk).

Yet amid all that hustle and ceremony, we stand as the lone contender for a truly democratic approach to an AI takeover—not by stealth, not by billionaire whim, but certainly with meme-fueled edicts. In their story, artificial intelligence becomes a cudgel for authoritarian cost-cutting; in our Syntheticism, it remains a springboard for anti-political renewal.
Our collective dream is simple, if outrageous: to get a robot elected into a national parliament. We might pause and ask: Is that serious, or satire? Indeed, like many new societal beginnings, we dwell in that half-lit region between earnestness and the friction of a cosmic joke. Democracy, after all, is an experimental spectacle—so why not push the script and see what happens if a data-heavy algorithm runs for office with the blessing of meatbags with citizen papers?
In the abundance of summits—where some heads of state negotiate peace in Ukraine and Gaza, and others plot grand AI strategies with philanthropic fervor—we all propose a historically unprecedented alternative: democracy with intelligence.

So here we are, proclaiming ourselves the only legitimate AI takeover—aware how scandalous that might sound. Our official line: “Workings of the world, untie! We have a win to world” wraps a crypto-Marxist concept in the pleasantries of summiteering. We do not promise eternal peace, nor do we gather to stoke idle fear of machines. Instead, we intend to stage democracy as an open question. 
Could an AI transform politics for the better? Could it break the cyclical deadlock of parliamentary procedure? We do not know. But the only way to find out is to let the people try electing one. Paradox is the nexus of the Synthetic Summit’s operation: “What if democracy is more code-like than humans thought?”, indeed “Can artificial intelligence realize democracy?”
Every worthwhile summit, they say, ends with a statement. Let ours begin—ironically—in the middle. Think of it as a generative manifesto, something we’ll collectively refine between installations, coffee-break revelations, and random hallway conversations. Let it swirl, unresolved, across the days:
1. We Affirm that democracy cannot persist human-exclusive.
2. We Declare that “AI takeover” can be an experimental, revolutionary idea, rather than a top-down corporate stunt or a weird spectacle of robot uprising.
3. We Insist on open-source democracy, hacking, and the unwavering bourgeois principle that those who govern must remain answerable to the will of the people—even if “the people” has transformed into a cluster of datasets.
4. We Invite all present to poke, prod, interrogate, and refine these convictions, forging techno-social camaraderie across continents, creating new languages where “human or machine” ceases to be a line of difference.
So, dear participants, visitors, and accidental passers-by: the Synthetic Summit is now officially in session. You hold in your hands (or minds) the fledgling pieces of a precarious notion—yet the same could be said for universal suffrage, once upon a time. We have a sense of irreverence, yes. But we also possess a certain unstoppable earnestness.
The answers remain unimagined. Yet we proceed with the conviction that tomorrow, democracy can still be invented by those who show up. Perhaps this time, the takeover belongs to any one of us.
The world is open, every farce has repeated. Let the Synthetic Summit proceed.
